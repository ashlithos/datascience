TL;DR — Null handling is the only cleaning fix with no correct default, so the review surface can't ask “do you approve my fix?” It has to ask “which answer are you willing to defend?” Seven surfaces below; I recommend Option 2 (consequence preview) — approve the number, not the method.

Problem

  • Every other cleaning fix has a right answer Maya is merely ratifying. Nulls don't. Drop, fill, and flag-and-keep produce different answers from the same data.
  • Real case in data/sample_customers.csv: 62 nulls in monthly_spend (5.1%) — 100% of them are plan='free', while free is only 62% of all rows. Textbook structured missingness.
  • Those 62 churn at 64.5% vs 52.8% overall. Dropping them quietly deletes the most at-risk segment.
  • The agent cannot resolve it alone. Whether free-plan spend is genuinely $0 or merely unrecorded is a business fact, not a statistical one.
  • Why now: profiler.py already diagnoses the mechanism and silently picks a fix. The diagnosis has no surface — so today the choice is invisible.

Options

Ordered by how far each moves what the user is approving — from a method (1) to a number (2) to a fact (3) to nothing-up-front (7).

On the figures: every monthly_spend number in these wireframes is real, computed from data/sample_customers.csv. It's the only column in that file with nulls — so other columns shown in the sketches (tenure_months, gender) are illustrative, standing in for the multi-column case.

  • Sorted by how much the choice moves the answer; reuses the “▸ Show the evidence” disclosure.
  • Approve / Choose a different fix / Skip — per column, never all-or-nothing.

One card per column, grouped by mechanism, with the impact stated in rows and columns — never a wall of nulls.

Trade-offs
  • Gives us consistency with the pattern Maya already learned in stage ① — but costs her vocabulary she doesn't own (“flag-and-keep”).
  • Gives us the smallest build (pure presentation over existing data) — but leaves the agent's recommendation authoritative rather than checkable.

Option 2 — Approve the consequence, not the method

Recommended
  • Compute the metric Maya actually asked for under every candidate strategy.
  • She picks the answer she's willing to defend. No imputation vocabulary required.
  • The spread is the headline — when strategies agree, the debate is visibly moot.

Maya reviews in the only currency she's fluent in — the metric. The agent's pick becomes checkable, not authoritative.

Trade-offs
  • Gives us the lowest trust gap of any option — but costs N× compute (one metric run per strategy). On FlowDash-sized data, free.
  • Gives us an honest view of when the choice doesn't matter — the same nulls move churn only 0.6pt (52.2% → 52.8%) — but needs the downstream metric known at prep time.
  • Converts an unanswerable question into an answerable one — but can't be a static card; it needs a compare view.

Option 3 — Ask the business question, not the statistics question

  • Nulls are usually a business fact wearing a statistics costume.
  • The agent asks the domain question and translates the answer into the fix itself.
  • “Not sure” is a first-class answer mapped to the conservative fix.

Maya isn't ratifying the agent's judgment — she's supplying the one fact it structurally lacks.

Trade-offs
  • Gives us the honest division of labour (agent brings the scan, human brings the domain) — but costs an interruption every time.
  • Gives us a fully deterministic result — the answer is the rule, and it's reusable — but only scales if something decides which columns are worth asking about (see Option 5).

  • Treatment options unlock only after Maya has seen why the values are absent.
  • Reserved for pattern: structured; diffuse cases skip straight to Option 1.

Deliberate friction where the analysis is fragile — the failure mode with nulls is a confident wrong answer accepted in one click.

Trade-offs
  • Gives us a takeover point while the framing is still open (“that's wrong, free users do get billed”) — but costs two screens per column.
  • Gives us an auditable diagnosis — but the structured/diffuse/too_few label is a heuristic, so its thresholds (share ≥70%, ≥25pt over baseline, n ≥20) must be on screen, not hidden in the code.

Option 5 — Sensitivity gate: only interrupt when the choice changes the answer

  • Run the metric under every strategy; compare the spread against a tolerance.
  • Under tolerance → the agent decides and footnotes it. Over → full approval card.
  • Same knob as the alert threshold: the threshold is the etiquette of autonomy.

Approving 11 identical-outcome decisions to reach the one that matters is how approval fatigue kills a gate.

Trade-offs
  • Gives us the lowest interrupt cost and spends Maya's attention only where it changes something — but costs the guarantee that she saw every decision.
  • Gives us a deterministic rule over probabilistic inputs (clean fit for a settings UI) — but requires the downstream metric at prep time, so it works for question-driven prep, not standalone pipeline runs.

Option 6 — Standing policy, ratified once

  • Per-run approval can't work for a digest that runs while Maya is asleep.
  • She ratifies a short policy sheet once; the agent applies it and logs every run.
  • Rule 5 is the escape hatch: anything uncovered still comes to her.

Trust shifts from per-decision to institutional: Maya trusts the policy, and the policy's track record.

Trade-offs
  • Gives us the only option that survives unattended runs — but silent application is exactly the failure mode iron rule “never silently clean” warns about.
  • Gives us a diffable config (a policy change becomes a reviewable event) — but is only safe paired with a visible, clickable decision log on every output.

Option 7 — Provisional apply with one-click revert

  • Don't block the answer. Apply the recommended default and keep Maya's flow intact.
  • A persistent chip rides every artifact the decision touched; changing it recomputes in place.
  • Freeze and require explicit approval once the result leaves the session.

Trades the gate for reversibility — defensible only because null handling is non-destructive by construction.

Trade-offs
  • Gives us zero interruption and a takeover point that's always available rather than offered once — but a number Maya already pasted into Slack can change underneath her.

My recommendation: Option 2. It's the only one that stops requiring Maya to have an opinion about imputation, and it makes the agent's recommendation checkable instead of authoritative — the same move the key-driver evidence disclosure made for confidence scores.

They compose rather than compete:

5 decides whether to ask → 4 shows why before offering a fix → 2 asks in the currency of the answer → 3 escalates to a plain-English business question when the mechanism is structured → 6 remembers the outcome as a rule → 7 keeps it reversible afterwards.

  • Confidence: Strong on Option 2 as the centrepiece; Genuinely torn on whether 5 or 6 is the right second build.
  • Reversal trigger: if prep turns out to run mostly unattended (digests, schedules) rather than question-driven, Option 2 loses its footing — it needs a known downstream metric — and Option 6 becomes the centrepiece instead.
